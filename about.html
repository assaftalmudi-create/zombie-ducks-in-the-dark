A bedtime audio game by Ashtray Studios

Zombie Ducks in the Dark

Zombie Ducks in the Dark invites you to close your eyes and follow the patter of webbed feet by ear, a playful echo of the left-right sound cues sometimes used in EMDR. Each short visit ends with the swamp falling quiet and the narrator wishing you good night, a natural moment to put the phone down.

The research behind the listening

How do you hear a duck on your left?

A footstep to your left reaches that ear a little sooner and can sound louder there. Middlebrooks and Green describe how listeners use both timing and level differences to locate sounds.

Middlebrooks & Green (1991) · Sound localization by human listeners

Can headphones put a sound straight ahead?

Wightman and Kistler compared real sound sources with headphone simulations tailored to each listener. The eight listeners placed most sounds similarly in both settings, though they confused front and back more often over headphones.

Wightman & Kistler (1989) · Headphone simulation of free-field listening, part II

What does EMDR have to do with the swamp?

The connection is the experience of shifting attention from one side to the other. In eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR), a therapist may use alternating tones as one part of a structured psychotherapy. Here, a sound’s position tells you where to throw your ball.

A pilot study by Servan-Schreiber and colleagues compared three patterns of tones and vibrations during EMDR sessions with 21 people with PTSD. Its findings concern those therapy sessions. The game’s health effects have not been established.

Servan-Schreiber et al. (2006) · Pilot study of stimulation patterns in EMDR
